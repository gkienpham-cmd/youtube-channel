## ACT 1 — The legend that refused to die (0:30–3:15)

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ JAXA footage of Hayabusa2; intercut a peregrine falcon diving. 📊 Lower-third: *Hayabusa2 — "Peregrine Falcon."*

To understand why this is so audacious, you have to understand the machine attempting it. Its name is Hayabusa2 — Japanese for "peregrine falcon," the fastest animal alive. And like its namesake, it was built to dive, snatch its target, and carry it home.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️/🎨 The original Hayabusa limping through space; then a streak of fire in a night sky.

Its older sibling, the original Hayabusa, was a beautiful disaster. Launched in 2003, it staggered to an asteroid and back on failing engines and leaking fuel — and when it finally reached Earth in 2010, the battered spacecraft burned up in the sky over the Australian outback, sacrificing itself to deliver one tiny capsule of dust. It was the first time humanity had ever brought back a piece of an asteroid.

Hayabusa2 was the sequel that got everything right.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ Real Ryugu imagery — the dark, spinning-top asteroid. 📊 "Ryugu — older than Earth."

In 2018, it arrived at a dark, diamond-shaped world called Ryugu — an asteroid as black as charcoal and older than the Earth itself. And what it did there sounds like science fiction.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ The hopping rovers / MASCOT lander; the SCI impactor animation; the touchdown plume.

It dropped tiny hopping robots onto the surface. The surface turned out to be a nightmare of boulders — nowhere safe to land — so the team improvised, dropping bright markers onto the asteroid just to give the spacecraft something to aim at. Then it fired a copper cannonball into Ryugu to blast open a fresh crater… and flew down into the debris and touched the surface with an accuracy of sixty centimeters. Less than the length of your arm — from across the solar system.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 Macro shot: dark grains in a sealed container, faint glints.

It collected a little over five grams of rock. But those few grams held something staggering: amino acids — the chemical building blocks of life — sealed inside material that hadn't changed in four and a half billion years.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ The 2020 Woomera capsule re-entry — a brilliant fireball, then a parachute in the desert dawn.
`[TEXT]` *December 2020*

In December 2020, it dropped its "treasure box" into the desert, and the world celebrated.

But here's the twist that sets up everything that follows.

Unlike its predecessor… Hayabusa2 didn't die.

`[SFX/MUSIC]` beat of quiet.

It had done everything so efficiently that it still had fuel in the tank. A perfectly healthy spacecraft, drifting through space, with nowhere left to be. So its engineers asked a dangerous question: what else could it do?

## ACT 2 — The job it was never built to survive (3:15–6:45)

`[VISUAL]` 📊 Title card: *Hayabusa2♯ — "Sharp."* Then the EAEEA trajectory line snaking out from Earth.

The answer became an extended mission called Hayabusa2 "Sharp" — and it points the falcon at a target that breaks every rule it was designed around.

Here's the problem.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 Side-by-side: a probe gently matching speed with an asteroid (relative speed → 0).

Everything Hayabusa2 did at Ryugu, it did slowly. It was built to *rendezvous* — to ease alongside an asteroid, matching its speed until the two were practically standing still next to each other, drifting together through space. Patience was its whole design.

Its new target demands the opposite of patience.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 A rock tumbling end over end; the probe screaming toward it. 📊 Counter spins up: *18,900 km/h.*
`[TEXT]` *July 5, 2026*

On July 5th, 2026, Hayabusa2 will pass an asteroid called Torifune — not by pulling alongside it, but by screaming past it at five and a quarter kilometers *every second*. Nearly nineteen thousand kilometers an hour. Fast enough to cross an entire country in the time it takes to read this sentence.

And it has to thread a needle.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 The 1-km miss-distance diagram against a ~450 m rotating asteroid.

To capture anything useful, it has to pass within about one kilometer of a rock roughly four hundred and fifty meters wide — a rock that's also tumbling, end over end, every five hours. Too far, and it sees nothing but a speck. Too close… and it doesn't survive the encounter at all.

Now here's the cruel part.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ New Horizons / a panning camera platform; then 📊 Hayabusa2's fixed, forward-bolted cameras.

A spacecraft built for flybys — like NASA's New Horizons, which visited Pluto — has cameras that swivel and pan, tracking their target as it races past. Hayabusa2 has nothing like that. Its cameras are bolted in place, staring straight ahead. It physically cannot turn fast enough to follow the asteroid.

So it has to aim at a point in empty space — where it *predicts* Torifune will be — and fire blind. Capturing everything in the few seconds of approach. Because the instant it flashes past closest approach, the asteroid drops out of frame… and it will never get a second look.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 Faint signal arcing across the solar system from Earth to the tiny probe. 📊 "Software upload — 2024."

To pull this off, engineers had to perform brain surgery from a hundred million kilometers away — uploading entirely new software in 2024 to teach an old spacecraft a skill it was never born with.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 Simple animation: a bullet blurring past a locked camera.

Picture it like this: you're trying to photograph a passing bullet — with a camera that can't turn — while *you* are moving faster than the bullet.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 Two failure states flash: a black shadow frame; a red collision flash.

And they nearly took the safe road instead — a gentler route through the inner solar system. They rejected it, because that path would have roasted the spacecraft too close to the Sun. The only way forward ran straight through this hair-raising flyby. Get the timing wrong by a few minutes, and Hayabusa2 photographs a shadow. Get the distance wrong by a hair… and the mission is over.

So why take the risk at all?

## ACT 3 — Why a tiny robot's gamble matters to you (6:45–9:45)

`[SFX/MUSIC]` warmer, rising. `[VISUAL]` 🎨 Pull back to Earth, city lights glowing.

Because this isn't really about one asteroid. It's a rehearsal. For us.

Remember the rock over Russia? Remember the dinosaurs? The whole reason this flyby exists is to help make sure that never happens to a city full of people.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 Map of tracked near-Earth asteroids lighting up — then a band of smaller ones that stay dark.

We track more than forty thousand near-Earth asteroids. But the city-killers — the ones between fifty and a hundred and fifty meters — are small enough to slip past our telescopes. We often don't see them until they're almost on top of us.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 The flyby precision morphs into an impact-and-deflection diagram.

So think about what Hayabusa2 is actually practicing. To fly within one kilometer of an asteroid at five kilometers a second, you need pinpoint, automatic, split-second targeting. Which is the *exact* same skill you'd need to do the opposite of a near-miss — to deliberately hit an asteroid and shove it off a collision course.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ Real NASA DART footage — the asteroid rushing up, then signal loss.
`[TEXT]` *NASA DART — 2022*

You may have seen this tested. In 2022, NASA flew a spacecraft called DART straight into a harmless asteroid on purpose — and measurably changed its orbit. For the first time, humanity proved we can move one.

But moving an asteroid is only half the battle. If we ever spot a real one heading our way with just a few years' warning, our first move would be to race a probe out to it — fast — and answer a single, life-or-death question: what is it made of? A solid slab of rock… or a loose pile of rubble held together by almost nothing? Because that changes everything about how you'd push it.

And racing out to a barely-known asteroid, on one high-speed pass, to scan it before it's gone… is exactly what Hayabusa2 is about to do at Torifune.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 The small probe against the vast dark — almost noble.

So this aging spacecraft, on a mission almost no one is watching, is quietly writing the playbook for how we might one day stop the next one. A dress rehearsal for saving a city. Maybe more.

Which raises the question it's flying all that way to answer: what *is* Torifune?

## ACT 4 — The mystery it's chasing (9:45–12:15)

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 A faint, flickering point of light against star-field — deliberately ambiguous, never a confident render.

And the honest answer, right now, is: we don't really know.

We've never seen it. To every telescope that's ever looked, Torifune is just a faint, shifting point of light. For over twenty years it didn't even have a name — only a code: 2001 CC21.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 Mythic "bird-boat" gliding through stars; 🎞️ children at a naming event if available. 📊 "Torifune — 'Heavenly Bird-Boat.'"

Then, in 2024, the team did something lovely. They opened the naming up to the public — and let a committee that included schoolchildren choose from over three thousand suggestions. The winner: Torifune. From *Ame-no-Torifune*, the "heavenly bird-boat" of Japanese myth — a divine vessel that sails the heavens with the speed of a bird and the steadiness of a rock. A quiet wish for a fast, safe passage.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 A light-curve graph spiking up and down as a lumpy shape rotates.

Here's what little we can read from that flickering dot. Torifune is a stony asteroid, the kind that's wandered in from the inner asteroid belt. But it brightens and dims so dramatically as it spins that it cannot be a simple sphere. It has to be strange — long, lopsided, stretched.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 Two asteroids drifting together into a single peanut shape.

Which leads to the tantalizing possibility scientists still can't rule out: that Torifune might be a *contact binary*. Two separate asteroids that drifted toward each other over eons, kissed at gentle speed, and fused — into a single, lumpy, peanut-shaped world.

Is it one rock… or two, frozen mid-embrace?

Nobody on Earth knows. And in a few short minutes, on July 5th… we finally will.
