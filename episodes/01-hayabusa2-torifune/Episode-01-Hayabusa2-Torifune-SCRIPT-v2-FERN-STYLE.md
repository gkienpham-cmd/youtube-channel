## BEAT 1 — The threat (0:40–2:00)

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ Real Chelyabinsk dashcam, Feb 2013 — a fireball brighter than the sun tears across the morning sky. `[SFX]` silence… then the delayed shockwave **boom** and car alarms.
`[TEXT]` *Russia, 2013*

To understand why this matters, go back to an ordinary morning in 2013.

The sky over a Russian city lights up brighter than the sun. A rock from space — about twenty meters wide, the size of a house — slams into the atmosphere and explodes with the energy of around thirty Hiroshima bombs. And nobody saw it coming. Not one telescope on Earth.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 Slow push on a dark asteroid drifting past a fragile, distant Earth.

Here's the part that should keep you up at night. That rock was *small*. The ones big enough to flatten an entire city — fifty to a hundred and fifty meters wide — are still out there. Drifting. Silent. Mostly undetected.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 A star map lights up with 40,000 tracked dots — then a faint cloud of *un*tracked ones flickers in behind them.

We track more than forty thousand near-Earth asteroids. But the small, dark ones slip right past our telescopes. We usually don't see them until they're almost on top of us.

And until very recently, even if we did… we had no real way to stop one.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 The small, scarred spacecraft cutting through deep space, sunlight raking across it.

That's the problem this aging spacecraft is quietly trying to solve.

## BEAT 2 — The machine (2:00–4:00)

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ JAXA footage of Hayabusa2; intercut a peregrine falcon folding into a dive. 📊 Lower-third: *Hayabusa2 · launched 2014 · 600 kg.*

This is Hayabusa2.

It's Japanese for "peregrine falcon" — the fastest animal alive — and like the bird, it was built to dive, snatch its target, and carry it home. Its ion engines are so gentle their whole thrust weighs about as much as a few coins in your palm.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️/🎨 The original Hayabusa limping through space; then a streak of fire across a night sky.

Its older sibling, the first Hayabusa, was a beautiful disaster. Launched in 2003, it limped to an asteroid and back on dying engines, then burned up over Australia in 2010 — sacrificing itself to deliver one capsule of dust. The first piece of an asteroid anyone had ever brought home.

Hayabusa2 was the sequel that got everything right.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ Real Ryugu imagery — the dark, spinning-top asteroid. 📊 *Ryugu — older than Earth.*

In 2018, it reached a dark, diamond-shaped world called Ryugu — an asteroid older than the Earth itself. And what it did there sounds made up.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ The copper-impactor animation; the touchdown plume.

It fired a copper cannonball into the surface to blast open a fresh crater. Then it flew down into the debris and touched the asteroid — with an accuracy of sixty centimeters. Less than the length of your arm… from across the solar system.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 Macro: dark grains in a sealed container, faint glints.

It collected just over five grams of rock. But those few grams held amino acids — the chemical building blocks of life — sealed inside material that hadn't changed in four and a half billion years.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ The 2020 Woomera capsule re-entry — a brilliant fireball, then a parachute at desert dawn. `[TEXT]` *December 2020*

In December 2020, it dropped that treasure into the desert — and the world celebrated.

But here's the twist.

`[SFX/MUSIC]` beat of quiet.

Unlike its predecessor… Hayabusa2 didn't die. It had worked so efficiently that it still had half its fuel left. A perfectly healthy spacecraft, drifting through space, with nowhere left to be.

So its engineers asked a dangerous question. What else could it do?

## BEAT 3 — The job it was never built to survive (4:00–6:15)

`[VISUAL]` 📊 Title card: *Hayabusa2♯ — "SHARP."* Then a trajectory line snaking out from Earth.

The answer became an extended mission with a fitting new name: Hayabusa2 SHARP. And it aims the falcon at a target that breaks every rule it was built around.

Here's the problem.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 A probe gently easing alongside an asteroid until both are still. *Relative speed → 0.*

Everything Hayabusa2 did at Ryugu, it did slowly. It was built to *rendezvous* — to ease alongside an asteroid and match its speed, until the two are practically parked side by side. Patience was the whole design.

Its new target demands the opposite.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 A rock tumbling end over end; the probe screaming in. 📊 Counter spins up: *18,900 km/h.* `[TEXT]` *July 5, 2026*

On July 5th, 2026, Hayabusa2 will pass an asteroid called Torifune — not by pulling alongside it, but by tearing past at more than five kilometers every second. And it has to thread a needle.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 The one-kilometer miss-distance diagram against a ~450 m rotating asteroid.

To capture anything useful, it has to pass within about one kilometer of a rock four hundred and fifty meters wide — a rock that's also tumbling, end over end, once every five hours. Too far, and it sees a blurry speck. Too close… and it doesn't survive.

Now here's the cruel part.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ A camera platform panning to track; then 📊 Hayabusa2's fixed, forward-bolted cameras.

A spacecraft built for flybys — like NASA's New Horizons at Pluto — has cameras that swivel to track a target as it races past. Hayabusa2's are bolted in place, staring straight ahead. It can't turn fast enough to follow the asteroid.

So it aims at empty space — where it predicts Torifune will be — and fires blind, grabbing everything in the last few seconds. The instant it flashes past, the rock is gone. No second look.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 A faint signal arcing from Earth across the solar system to the tiny probe. 📊 *Software upload — 2024.*

To pull this off, engineers performed brain surgery from a hundred million kilometers away — uploading brand-new software in 2024 to teach an old spacecraft a skill it was never born with.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 Simple animation: a bullet blurring past a locked camera.

Picture it like this. You're trying to photograph a passing bullet — with a camera that can't turn — while *you're* moving faster than the bullet.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 Two failure states flash: a black shadow frame; a red collision flash.

So why take the risk at all?

## BEAT 4 — Why it matters to you (6:15–7:45)

`[SFX/MUSIC]` warmer, rising. `[VISUAL]` 🎨 Pull back to Earth, city lights glowing.

Because this was never really about one asteroid. It's a rehearsal. For us.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 The flyby precision morphs into an impact-and-deflection diagram.

Think about what it's actually practicing. To fly within one kilometer of an asteroid at five kilometers a second, you need pinpoint, split-second targeting — the *exact* skill you'd need to do the opposite: to deliberately slam into an asteroid and shove it off a collision course.

`[VISUAL]` 🎞️ Real NASA DART footage — the asteroid rushing up, then signal loss. `[TEXT]` *NASA DART — 2022*

In 2022, NASA flew a spacecraft called DART straight into a harmless asteroid, on purpose — and measurably changed its orbit. For the first time, humanity moved another world.

But moving an asteroid is only half the battle.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 A probe racing out toward a dim, unknown rock.

If we ever spot a real one heading our way with only a few years' warning, our first move is to race a probe out to it — fast — and answer one question. What's it made of? Solid rock? Or a loose pile of rubble held together by almost nothing? Because that changes everything about how you'd push it.

And racing out to a barely-known asteroid, on a single high-speed pass, to scan it before it's gone… is exactly what Hayabusa2 is about to do at Torifune.

## BEAT 5 — The mystery: what *is* Torifune? (7:45–9:00)

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 A faint, flickering point of light against a star-field — deliberately ambiguous, never a confident render.

But the strangest part of this whole story is the asteroid itself.

Because right now, we don't really know what Torifune is. We've never seen it. To every telescope that's ever looked, it's nothing but a faint, shifting dot of light. For more than twenty years, it didn't even have a name — only a code. 2001 CC21.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 A mythic "bird-boat" gliding through stars. 📊 *Torifune — "Heavenly Bird-Boat."*

Then, in 2024, the team did something lovely. They let the public name it — schoolchildren on the committee — choosing from over three thousand suggestions. The winner: Torifune. The "heavenly bird-boat" of Japanese myth — a divine ship that sails the heavens with the speed of a bird and the steadiness of a rock. A quiet wish for a fast, safe passage.

`[VISUAL]` 📊 A light-curve graph spiking up and down as a lumpy shape rotates.

Here's the little we can read from that flickering dot. Torifune is a stony asteroid. But it brightens and dims so dramatically as it spins that it cannot be a simple ball. It has to be strange — long, lopsided, stretched.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 Two asteroids drifting together into a single peanut shape.

Which leaves a tantalizing possibility no one can rule out. That Torifune is actually *two* asteroids — two separate rocks that drifted together over millions of years, kissed at gentle speed, and fused into a single, lumpy, peanut-shaped world.

Is it one rock… or two, frozen mid-embrace?

`[SFX/MUSIC]` quiet.

Nobody on Earth knows. And in a few short minutes, on July 5th… we finally will.

`[SFX/MUSIC]` tense, ticking. `[VISUAL]` 🎨 The probe and asteroid closing; a countdown clock ticking down.

After a journey of years, this is what it all comes down to. A single pass. A few minutes of data, gathered blind, at almost nineteen thousand kilometers an hour. And then Torifune is behind it, shrinking into the dark.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 The falcon flies on, outward, past the asteroid.

And if it survives — if it threads the needle — the falcon doesn't turn for home. It keeps going. Toward a target even stranger than this one.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 A tiny rock spinning fast; a school bus beside it for scale. 📊 *1998 KY26 — arrival 2031.*

Its true final destination is an asteroid the size of a school bus, barely bigger than the spacecraft itself — spinning so fast it turns once every five minutes. When Hayabusa2 reaches it in 2031, it'll be the smallest, fastest world we've ever dared to visit.

But that's a story for another day.

`[SFX/MUSIC]` swell to awe. `[VISUAL]` 🎨 Wide, beautiful: the probe small against a star-filled void.

For now, just think about what's out there. A machine that already made history — that already came home a hero — chose to go back into the dark. Not to explore. Not for glory. But to learn how to protect the planet that built it.

`[VISUAL]` 🎨 Slow push on Earth, small and blue.

So the next time you hear that an asteroid "safely passed" Earth… remember this. Somewhere out there, a tiny falcon is flying — practicing, in silence, for the day one of them *doesn't* pass safely.

And quietly making sure we're ready.
